# Learning Patterns: Make It Stick

## Pattern 1: Active Retrieval (Self-Quizzing)
**When to use**: As your primary study method for any subject.
**How**:
1. Pause while reading and ask yourself conceptual questions (e.g., "What is the core idea?").
2. Cover your notes and try to state the definitions or principles from memory.
3. Actually write or speak the answer; don't just think "I know that."
4. Check your answers immediately to correct errors (feedback loop).
**Trade-offs**: It feels slower and more frustrating than rereading, but leads to far superior long-term retention.

## Pattern 2: Spaced Retrieval Schedule
**When to use**: When you need to retain a large body of information over a semester or a career.
**How**:
1. Practice new material within 24 hours of first exposure.
2. Review again after a few days.
3. Review again after one week, then one month.
4. If you miss an answer, move it back to more frequent review.
**Trade-offs**: Requires disciplined scheduling and planning ahead of time.

## Pattern 3: Interleaving (Mixed Practice)
**When to use**: When learning subjects with multiple problem types (Math, Science, Music, Sports).
**How**:
1. Instead of doing 20 "Type A" problems, do: 1A, 1B, 1C, 2A, 2B, 2C.
2. Force yourself to identify the "type" before selecting the solution.
3. If using flashcards, shuffle them so you never know which topic is coming next.
**Trade-offs**: Performance during practice will be lower and feel "clunky," but final mastery will be much higher.

## Pattern 4: Elaboration (Meaning-Making)
**When to use**: When learning abstract concepts or complex systems.
**How**:
1. Relate new information to something you already know (e.g., "This is like...").
2. Create a metaphor or visual image for the concept.
3. Explain the concept to a "rubber duck" or a peer in your own words.
4. Draw a "summary sheet" showing how different parts of a system connect.
**Trade-offs**: Requires more cognitive energy up front to find relevant connections.

## Pattern 5: Generative Problem Solving
**When to use**: Before a lecture or before reading the "solution" in a text.
**How**:
1. Attempt to solve a problem *before* you've been taught the method.
2. Even if you fail, you "prime" your brain to notice the critical details of the correct solution when it's provided.
3. Explain the key ideas you *expect* to find in a text before you open it.
**Trade-offs**: Can be frustrating if you value "correctness" over "process," but it significantly increases encoding strength.

## Pattern 6: Reflection (The After-Action Review)
**When to use**: After a lecture, meeting, surgery, or any significant learning experience.
**How**:
1. Ask: "What happened? What did I do? How did it work out?"
2. Ask: "What would I do differently next time? What do I need to learn to improve?"
3. Write a "learning paragraph" connecting the experience to your existing mental models.
**Trade-offs**: Requires a habit of "slowing down" after action to process the experience.

## Pattern 7: Mnemonic Memory Palace
**When to use**: For arbitrary lists, dates, or sequences that lack inherent structure.
**How**:
1. Visualize a familiar building (your house, your school).
2. Choose a "path" through the building.
3. Associate each item to be remembered with a vivid, unusual image placed at a specific "station" along the path.
4. Walk the path mentally to retrieve the items.
**Trade-offs**: High initial effort to build the "palace," but provides nearly perfect recall for ordered information.

## Pattern 8: Peer Instruction
**When to use**: In classroom or group study settings.
**How**:
1. Grapple with a concept individually for a few minutes.
2. Join a small group and explain your understanding to others.
3. Try to convince those who disagree with you.
4. Use the group consensus to calibrate your own understanding.
**Trade-offs**: Requires a social setting and openness to having your views challenged.
