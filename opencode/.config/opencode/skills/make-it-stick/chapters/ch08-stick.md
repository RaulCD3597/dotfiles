# Chapter 8: Make It Stick

## Core Idea
The ability to learn is a mastered skill, not an innate gift. By taking charge of your own education and following disciplined, evidence-based strategies—primarily retrieval, spacing, and interleaving—you can significantly increase your intellectual capability and achieve mastery in any field.

## Practical Strategies for Students
- **Retrieval Practice (Self-Quizzing)**:
  - Pause while reading to ask: "What are the key ideas?" "How would I define these terms?"
  - Use chapter-end questions and generate your own test questions.
  - Set aside time every week to quiz yourself on both new and old material.
- **Spaced Practice**:
  - Establish a schedule of self-quizzing with time intervals between sessions.
  - Don't drop "mastered" flashcards; shuffle them back in and review them periodically (e.g., monthly).
- **Interleaved Practice**:
  - Mix up problem types or specimens during study sessions rather than practicing one type until you "have it down."
  - Constantly challenge your ability to recognize the problem type and select the right solution.
- **Elaboration**:
  - Connect new material to existing knowledge.
  - Use metaphors or visual images (e.g., visualize an atom as a solar system).
  - Create "summary sheets" that graphically illustrate how different systems interrelate.
- **Generation**:
  - Try to answer questions or solve problems before the solution is provided.
  - Explain key ideas you *expect* to find in a text before reading it.
- **Reflection**:
  - Review experiences by asking: "What went well? What could have gone better? What does this remind me of?"
  - Write weekly "learning paragraphs" to connect class concepts to real-life applications.
- **Calibration**:
  - Use objective instruments (quizzes, practice tests) to identify "illusions of knowing."
  - Actually write out the answers to practice questions rather than assuming "I know that."

## Key Concepts
- **Taking Charge**: Successful learners are active, not passive. They take responsibility for identifying what they don't know and finding ways to learn it.
- **Trusting the Process**: Evidence-based strategies often feel slower and more frustrating than traditional methods. Learners must "trust the process" and persist despite the initial discomfort.
- **Metacognitive Calibration**: Using frequent testing to keep your internal sense of mastery aligned with your actual performance.

## Case Studies of Success
- **Michael Young (Medical Student)**: Rose from the bottom of his class to the top by replacing passive rereading with active retrieval practice, visualization, and self-quizzing.
- **Timothy Fellows (Psychology Student)**: Achieved near-perfect grades by reading before lectures, answering rhetorical questions in class, and creating his own study guides from scratch.
- **Mary Pat Wenderoth (Professor)**: Encourages student mastery through "summary sheets" (elaboration) and "learning paragraphs" (reflection).

## Key Takeaways
1. Mastering the ability to learn is the most important skill for long-term success.
2. Effortful learning (retrieval, spacing, interleaving) is the only way to build durable, transferable knowledge.
3. Intuition is a poor guide for learning; what *feels* effective (rereading) usually isn't, and what *feels* hard (retrieval) is most beneficial.
4. Setbacks and errors are essential components of striving and building expertise.
5. Intellectual ability lies largely within your own control through the disciplined application of learning strategies.
