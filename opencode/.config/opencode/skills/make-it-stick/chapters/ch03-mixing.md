# Chapter 3: Mix Up Your Practice

## Core Idea
The most common form of practice—massed repetition—is a myth that leads to fast gains but quick forgetting. True mastery and versatility come from practice that is spaced, interleaved, and varied, even though these methods feel slower and more difficult in the moment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Spaced Practice**: Breaking study or practice into separate periods with time intervals between them. This allows for memory consolidation and makes the next retrieval attempt more effortful and effective.
- **Interleaved Practice**: Switching between two or more subjects or skills during a single practice session. This forces the brain to constantly "reload" the information and helps you learn to choose the right strategy for different problem types.
- **Varied Practice**: Practicing a skill in different contexts or with different parameters (e.g., throwing beanbags at different distances). This builds a broader, more flexible "schema" for applying the skill.
- **The Leitner System**: A method of spaced repetition using flashcard boxes where the cards you know well are practiced less frequently, while those you struggle with are moved to boxes that are reviewed more often.

## Key Concepts
- **Massed Practice**: The "practice-practice-practice" of conventional wisdom. It builds "momentary strength" but not "underlying habit strength."
- **Consolidation**: The process where the brain strengthens memory traces, gives them meaning, and connects them to prior knowledge. This process takes hours or days and is aided by sleep.
- **Discrimination Skills**: The ability to assess a situation or problem and select the correct tool or solution from a variety of options.
- **Habit Strength vs. Momentary Strength**: Massed practice creates high momentary strength (ease in the moment) but low habit strength (durable memory). Spacing/interleaving does the opposite.
- **Blocked Practice**: A form of practice where one skill is repeated over and over in the same context before moving to the next (e.g., always hitting 20-foot putts). It is often mistaken for varied practice.

## Mental Models
- **Binge-and-Purge**: Think of cramming as binge-eating information that is quickly "purged" (forgotten) right after the exam.
- **The Rink position**: Practicing a move from the same spot on the ice is like throwing beanbags into a "three-foot bucket"—it limits your ability to adjust to real-world variations.
- **Familiarity Trap**: The dangerous feeling of "knowing" something just because it feels familiar, which leads to mental shortcuts and skipping necessary practice.

## Anti-patterns
- **Single-minded focus**: Concentrating on one thing until it feels "nailed" before moving on.
- **Summer Boot Camps**: Condensed, high-intensity training that lacks the spacing necessary for durable retention.
- **Clustered Math Problems**: Textbooks that give you 20 versions of the same problem type in a row, which removes the need for discrimination.

## Key Takeaways
1. Spacing out practice feels less productive because some forgetting has set in, but the extra effort required to retrieve the information makes the learning stronger.
2. Interleaving different problem types improves final performance significantly (up to 215% better in some studies) compared to blocked practice.
3. Variety in practice helps you transfer learning to unfamiliar situations and develop a more flexible "vocabulary" of responses.
4. "Practice like you play": The most effective practice reflects the way the knowledge or skill will be used in the real world.
5. Reflection (What happened? How did it work? What next?) is a high-value form of spaced retrieval and elaboration.
